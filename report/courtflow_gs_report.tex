\begin{abstract}
\method turns synchronized videos from cameras around a basketball court into a 3D scene that can
be viewed from a new camera angle. It first builds one clean, static ``starting scene'' from frame
0. It then follows each player through the video by moving small sets of control points, and
occasionally improves visual detail. On one RTX PRO 6000 it reconstructs all 700 frames from 12 of the 36 cameras
in about two hours, and the 24 cameras it never sees score 20.4~dB PSNR on average over the whole
video. The result is a 3D reconstruction, novel-view videos, and a saved Gaussian model that can be
rendered from any camera without retraining.
\end{abstract}

\section*{TL;DR}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The task.} Rebuild a 28-second basketball game (700 frames) in 3D from 12 of its 36
cameras, so it can be watched from any angle. Quality is judged only on the other 24 cameras, which
the method never sees.
\item \textbf{The idea.} Few cameras cannot pin down millions of freely moving points, so the method
keeps the unknowns small. It builds frame 0 carefully once: each player is found in 3D and given a
3D shape from masks and matched points. After that, every player moves by about a thousand control
points, and every tenth frame the whole scene is refreshed. The court and crowd are built once and
only lightly updated.
\item \textbf{The results.} On one RTX PRO 6000 at $1920\times1080$, the whole game takes about two
hours from raw videos to finished novel-view videos. The unseen cameras score 20.4~dB PSNR on average
over all 700 frames. A strong existing method, ClipGStream, scored 17.2--17.9~dB on the same cameras,
and only on the first 10 frames. A new view renders in 2--3~ms, over 300 frames per second even at 4K.
\item \textbf{What we learned.} Longer training made the existing method \emph{worse} on unseen
cameras, which shows it memorizes the 12 views. Our first full 700-frame run revealed that the scene
kept growing until it fell apart, and capping its size made it faster, smaller and better at the same
time. Training at native 4K was slower and did not beat the 1080p working resolution.
\item \textbf{What is still weak.} Players in crowded plays late in the game become blurred or broken
up, while the court and crowd stay clean. Better tracking of overlapping players is the next step.
\item \textbf{Using it.} Playback needs no neural networks, only moving the points and drawing them.
It can be served like cloud gaming (render on a server, stream video) or like game downloads (send
compressed scene data, draw on the viewer's device).
\end{itemize}


\section{Problem and data}
The data contains 36 synchronized 4K cameras placed in a ring around the court. Their positions,
orientations, and lenses have already been calibrated, so the system knows exactly where every
camera is. We train with every third camera (views $0,3,\ldots,33$) and keep the remaining 24
cameras aside for an honest test from viewpoints the model did not see during training. The camera
calibration is fixed throughout the method.

The input adapter reads the 36 videos and the COLMAP calibration (\texttt{cameras.txt} and
\texttt{images.txt}) and creates an undistorted working copy for training. By default this copy is
half resolution ($1920\times1080$), which keeps the camera geometry while making the many repeated
training renders cheap; the same pipeline also runs at the native $3840\times2160$.

\section{Existing methods and the 12-view problem}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{How other methods handle moving scenes.}
A Gaussian splatting scene is built from millions of small, coloured blobs called Gaussians. To show
motion, a method has to decide how those blobs move from one video frame to the next. Most recent
methods let every blob move on its own:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dynamic 3D Gaussians}~\cite{luiten2024dynamic} stores a new position and rotation for
every blob at every frame, with a penalty that keeps neighbouring blobs moving together.
\item \textbf{3DGStream}~\cite{sun2024_3dgstream} trains a small neural network for each frame that
says how every blob moves.
\item \textbf{QUEEN}~\cite{girish2024queen} stores a compressed per-blob change for each frame.
\item \textbf{ClipGStream}~\cite{liang2026clipgstream} splits the video into short clips and learns
the motion of each clip together.
\item \textbf{SC-GS}~\cite{huang2024scgs} is closest to our method: a few control points move the
blobs. But it learns the motion with a neural network trained on the whole video at once, and it
was built for dense or single-camera video.
\end{itemize}

All of these were tested with many more cameras than this assignment gives us
(Table~\ref{tab:related}). The closest test is ClipGStream's ``Long 360'' scene: a ring of 36 4K
cameras, just like ours, but it trains on 32 of them. We may train on only 12. With 12 cameras,
neighbouring cameras are $30^\circ$ apart, and a distant player may be clearly visible in only a
few of them. When every blob can move freely, the method can match those few images exactly while
putting blobs in the wrong 3D place. The result looks right from the training cameras and wrong
from any new camera.

\paragraph{What \method does differently.}
\method was designed for few cameras from the start:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Far fewer things to solve for.} A player's blobs cannot move freely. They follow 1024
control points, and each player first moves as a whole, then adds small movements for arms and
legs, while keeping its shape. Twelve cameras are enough to pin down a few thousand control points;
they are not enough to pin down millions of free blobs.
\item \textbf{Shape comes from more than colour.} With few cameras, many wrong 3D shapes can produce
the right colours. \method first builds each player's rough 3D shape from where the player appears
in every camera (the ``visual hull'') and from matched points between cameras. It also randomly
hides blobs during training (DropGaussian~\cite{park2025dropgaussian}) so no single blob can
memorize one view.
\item \textbf{Each player is kept separate.} Every player has their own control points, so one
player can never drag a teammate or the floor along. When the automatic masks mix two players up,
the mistake is fixed by checking which player is where in 3D.
\item \textbf{The still parts are done once.} Players cover only about 4\% of each image; the rest is
court and crowd that never moves. Other methods re-fit that area every frame. \method builds it
once at frame 0 and afterwards only updates the players. This makes it both faster (less work per
frame) and better (the court cannot slowly drift out of place).
\end{itemize}
The catch is a slower start: building the first frame carefully (Stage A) takes longer than a plain
3D Gaussian start. But that happens once per scene; after it, each frame takes a few seconds.

\section{Methodology}
Before doing anything else, every image is undistorted. This removes lens bending, so a straight
camera ray in the mathematics corresponds to a straight ray in the image. In the notation used
later, camera $c$ is $P_c=K_c[R_c\mid t_c]$, with world-to-camera conversion $x_c=R_cx+t_c$.
Readers do not need this equation to follow the method: it only says that the known calibration
projects a 3D point into each image. Camera poses, lens parameters, and per-camera brightness
corrections are kept fixed.

\subsection{Frame-0 Reconstruction}
\paragraph{Masks and 3D identities:}
Photometric reconstruction alone is too ambiguous for the moving players. Each player occupies a
small portion of a frame, shares colour and texture with teammates, and is frequently overlapped
by another player or various structures / elements in the scene. 
Foreground masks tell the system both ``which pixels belong to this person for geometry matching''
and ``where this person is allowed to appear when rendered.''
This prevents background splats from being absorbed into players and prevents players from
growing fuzzy opaque halos onto the court. It also tells the optimizer which
Gaussian/control-point group should explain each pixel.  This prevents a global reconstruction
from improving RGB error by assigning one player's appearance or motion to another player or to
the background.

Simply running a detector in each camera is not enough: ``person 4'' in one image has no reason to
be the same physical person as ``person 4'' in another. We first tried matching 2D mask centres
and colours between cameras. With cameras about $30^\circ$ apart, that split 68 masks into 60
supposed people. Full-frame MASt3R matching was also too coarse for small players and made
unreliable star-shaped rays. Instead, CourtFlow-GS establishes identity \emph{in 3D first}.

Faster R-CNN proposes person boxes (score $\geq0.5$), and SAM2 turns each box into a precise
foreground mask. We use agreement between those masks to make rough 3D blobs, where each blob is
a likely physical player. We project each blob back into every camera and match it to the local
SAM2 mask. When a mask is missing, the projected blob tells SAM2 where to look again. In this way,
noisy per-camera masks become one consistent player ID across the ring.

Concretely, we cover the court with a 2.4 m-high box that extends 1 m beyond the court lines, and
divide it into small cubes called voxels. A voxel is kept only if enough cameras see it inside a
person mask: at least $\max(4,n_{\mathrm{visible}}-2)$ cameras. Put simply, several cameras must
agree, but up to two are allowed to disagree because a player can be hidden. We discard blobs that
are too small, too short, or floating above the floor. A blob wide enough to contain two people is
split on the court floor.

For a missing camera mask, we accept the new SAM2 result only if its colour is similar to that same
3D player in the other cameras. If it still looks wrong, we mark that player as \emph{unknown} in
that camera, not as background. This is important: a hidden or missed player should not teach the
model that the court is visible there. Finally, if two accepted masks overlap, the closer player
owns the pixel. The result is one label image $L_c$ per camera in which every pixel is either
background or exactly one player. Later, this lets every player have separate Gaussian splats and
separate motion controls.

\paragraph{Occlusion-aware visual hull.}
A visual hull is a rough 3D body made by intersecting the player silhouettes from several cameras.
The usual rule says to remove a voxel if even one camera does not see the player there. That works
for a clean object on a turntable, but fails in basketball: another player, the hoop support, or
padding can hide a real arm, leg, or head. We saw those missing body parts with a strict hull.

The opposite rule (forgiving any missing view) was also bad. One allowed miss made player hulls
about $2.5\times$ too large. Our compromise asks one simple question: \emph{is there measured
evidence that something closer to this camera was blocking the player?} We forgive a missing pixel
only when the answer is yes. This is what ``occlusion-aware'' means here.

For each player, we test a fine 1 cm grid inside the rough 3D blob, with a small margin. Camera
$c$ votes against a candidate voxel $x$ only when the voxel is visible in that camera, the player
has a trusted label there, the projected pixel is not labelled as that player, and nothing nearer
explains why. A nearer explanation can be another labelled player or a measured background surface
(such as the hoop support) at least 30 cm in front. The background depth map $D_c$ comes from
reliable full-frame MASt3R background points that agree in at least two additional cameras. We
retain the nearest depth at each image location and ignore floor points within 0.5 m of the court.

We keep $x$ when at least two cameras see it as the player and no camera has an \emph{unexplained}
reason to reject it. Voxels below the floor are then removed. A camera where the player is unknown
casts no vote. Thus the method does not keep geometry merely because occlusion might be possible;
it keeps it only when a nearer player or surface actually supports that explanation.

\paragraph{Masked crop geometry and Gaussian seeds.}
Feature matching across a full image works for the court, but a distant player may occupy only
about $6\times25$ pixels once MASt3R shrinks the whole frame to 512 pixels. Our full-frame player
matches made a radial ``star'' of unreliable 3D rays. The masks solve this by giving the player
the full matching resolution: for nearby camera pairs, we crop a padded square around the same 3D
player and resize that crop to $512\times512$. MASt3R searches only inside the source player mask.

After mapping matches back into the original camera images, we retain only confident matches that
are in front of the cameras, reproject accurately, use cameras far enough apart for useful depth,
lie inside a slightly expanded player hull, and agree with at least one more labelled camera. This
removes the court and background rays that slipped through earlier mask-only tests.

The retained matches are accurate surface seeds. Hull voxels fill only textureless gaps more than
two hull voxels away from a seed. We colour points only from cameras that agree with the label and
can actually see the point, remove outliers, merge nearby player points at 5 mm and background
points at 10 cm, and turn the result into oriented, elongated Gaussian splats. Each Gaussian stores
position, shape, opacity, and simple view-dependent colour (degree-1 spherical harmonics).
Players train for 7000 iterations (four cameras per step) and background for 10000 (two cameras
per step), using
\[
\mathcal L_{\rm image}=\|I-\hat I\|_1+0.2\,\mathrm{D\mbox{-}SSIM}
+0.1\mathcal L_{\rm alpha}+0.05\mathcal L_{\rm depth}.
\]
This loss asks the rendered image to match the real image, its structure, its silhouette, and its
initial depth. The depth term fades out halfway through training, after it has helped establish the
coarse shape. Starting at iteration 500, we add detail where needed every 100 iterations until
halfway through training. We remove nearly transparent splats and splats that land outside an
expanded player mask in two cameras.

\subsection{Group-aware control-point MLS}
\paragraph{Control Groups}
After frame 0, the task is to move the same Gaussian splats as players move. Rather than moving
millions of splats independently, we place a much smaller set of control points on each player.
Moving a control point moves nearby splats smoothly. This is called moving-least-squares (MLS)
deformation.

A single global control graph is reasonable for one connected object, but unsafe in a crowded game.
Controls on two nearby players can otherwise explain the same image error, causing one player to
pull another player's splats or the background. CourtFlow-GS treats every identity as a separate
motion group. This keeps the useful smooth motion of MLS while forbidding cross-player influence.

Every dynamic Gaussian belongs to exactly one person; the ball, when enabled, is another group,
and background is group 0. We choose controls spread evenly over each object: 1024 per person,
16 for the ball, and 1000 for background. Each Gaussian is permanently connected to its eight
nearest controls from the \emph{same} group. Closer controls get more influence through the fixed
radial weights
\[
w_{ij}=\frac{\exp(-\|x_i-p_j\|^2/(2\sigma_j^2))}
{\sum_{\ell=1}^{8}\exp(-\|x_i-p_\ell\|^2/(2\sigma_\ell^2))},
\]
where $\sigma_j$ is the average distance to control $j$'s three nearest controls. The six nearest
same-group controls form the shape-preserving ARAP graph. In plain terms, a player's splats follow
that player's nearby controls, never a teammate's or the court's.

\paragraph{Shared Translation + Local Motion}
Trying to move every control independently made weakly observed controls lag or drift. By frame 10
that version reached 0.60 mask overlap (IoU), compared with 0.71 when each player first gets one
shared translation and then small local offsets. The shared translation handles running across the
court; the local offsets handle arms, legs, and other articulation. A rigid local MLS transform
and the ARAP shape loss keep neighbouring splats from stretching apart.

At frame $f$, controls begin from the previous frame's velocity. We optimize one shared translation
$T_k$ per moving group and small local offsets $\delta_j$ for 50 Adam iterations, using four
cameras per iteration. Their learning rates are 0.020 m and 0.003 m. Let
$t_j=T_{g(j)}+\delta_j$ and $p'_j=p_j+t_j$. For Gaussian $i$, the fixed weights above blend nearby
original controls $p_j$ and moved controls $p'_j$ to form a best local rotation $R_i$:
\[
 \bar p_i=\sum_jw_{ij}p_j,\quad \bar q_i=\sum_jw_{ij}p'_j,\quad
 M_i=\sum_jw_{ij}(p_j-\bar p_i)(p'_j-\bar q_i)^T.
\]
With $M_i=USV^T$, set $R_i=VU^T$, flipping the last column of $V$ if $\det(VU^T)<0$. The final
Gaussian position is $x'_i=R_i(x_i-\bar p_i)+\bar q_i$; its orientation and simple colour direction
are rotated too. A fused CUDA operation performs this transform and its backward pass. The reported
run does not separately optimize control rotations.

Every fifth frame, we render where each player is expected, prompt SAM2 with that box and centre,
and assign overlapping pixels to the strongest rendered player. We triangulate the 2D mask centres
from several cameras into a rough 3D player centre. This is a \emph{motion anchor}: it gives the
shared translation a reliable hint about where a player moved, especially after fast motion or
occlusion. If enough cameras see the player, a robust Huber anchor of scale 0.3 m and weight 0.1
is used. Between those frames, no made-up mask or anchor is used. The deformation objective is
\[
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\rm image}+0.3\mathcal L_{\rm BCE}^{\rm instance}
+\mathcal L_{\rm ARAP}+0.01\mathcal L_{\rm accel}+\mathcal L_{\rm anchor},
\]
Here the image term matches appearance, BCE matches each player's rendered mask, ARAP preserves
local shape, acceleration discourages sudden jitter, and the anchor discourages global drift.
Background controls stay frozen while the moving splats are rendered over a cached background.

\subsection{Focused refinement}
MLS provides stable, smooth motion, but deliberately does not change a Gaussian's colour, size, or
amount of detail. Updating the full image after every video frame would be wasteful: players cover
less than 1\% of the court image, so most compute would repeatedly optimize the static floor. We
therefore refine only the moving-player crops most frames, and use occasional full-image keyframes
to correct drift and add a limited amount of new detail.

After every deformation, we save (``bake'') the moved Gaussian and control state for that frame.
On a normal frame, all moving splats are projected into each training camera and surrounded by one
box with 48 pixels of padding. The box is aligned to the rasterizer's 16-pixel tiles, and the camera
principal point is shifted so rendering the crop is exactly equivalent to rendering that part of
the full image. We run 100 refinement iterations on two crops per step. No splats are added or
removed here, so their control bindings stay stable.

Every tenth frame is a keyframe. It instead runs 500 full-image iterations, can add splats from
iteration 50 every 50 iterations until 60\% of the schedule, and limits growth to 5\% per keyframe and to 2 million Gaussians in total (Figure~\ref{fig:drift} shows why the total cap matters).
It then reconnects each Gaussian to its eight nearest same-group controls and refreshes the cached
background. Held-out cameras are never included in any training loss.

\subsection{Pseudocode}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:courtflow} summarizes the whole process. The helper names are intentionally
literal: first build the frame-0 scene, then for each video frame estimate motion, bake that motion,
and improve either player crops or a periodic full keyframe.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{CourtFlow-GS online reconstruction}\label{alg:courtflow}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Frame-0 images, synchronized training videos, fixed calibration
\State $(\mathit{state},\mathit{controls}) \gets \textsc{BuildCanonicalScene}(\mathit{frame}_0,\mathit{calibration})$
\For{each $f$ in the video sequence}
  \State $\mathit{images} \gets \textsc{DecodeUndistortNormalize}(\mathit{trainingVideos},f)$
  \If{$f \bmod 5 = 0$}
    \State $\mathit{masks} \gets \textsc{RepairMultiViewSAM2Identities}(\mathit{images},\mathit{state.dynamic})$
    \State $\mathit{anchors} \gets \textsc{TriangulateMaskCentroids}(\mathit{masks})$
  \Else
    \State $\mathit{masks},\mathit{anchors} \gets \emptyset,\emptyset$
  \EndIf
  \State $\mathit{offsets} \gets \textsc{OptimizeControls}(L_1{+}\mathrm{D\text{-}SSIM},\mathit{masks},\mathit{anchors},\mathrm{ARAP})$
  \State $\mathit{state} \gets \textsc{BakeFusedRigidMLS}(\mathit{state},\mathit{controls},\mathit{offsets})$
  \If{$f \bmod 10 \ne 0$}
    \State $\mathit{state} \gets \textsc{RefineDynamicCrops}(\mathit{state},\mathit{images},100)$
  \Else
    \State $\mathit{state} \gets \textsc{RefineFullImages}(\mathit{state},\mathit{images},500)$
    \State $\mathit{controls} \gets \textsc{Rebind}(\mathit{state},\mathit{controls})$; \textsc{CacheBackground}($\mathit{state}$)
  \EndIf
  \State \textsc{SaveDiagnostics}($\mathit{state}$)
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Execution Walkthrough}
The previous section explains \emph{why} each part of the method exists. This section follows one
complete run from the raw capture to the final videos: which program runs, what it reads, what it
writes, and where time is spent. Everything is started by a single command,
\begin{verbatim}
python -m ring_init.e2e --scene basketball --dataset-dir <capture> \
    --config config.local.json --out-dir <out> --frames 0:700
\end{verbatim}
where the capture directory holds the 36 videos (\texttt{cameras/view\_XXX.mp4}) and the COLMAP
calibration (\texttt{calibration/}). The local configuration only names the SAM2 checkpoint; all
other settings are the defaults, and the exact resolved configuration is written to
\texttt{e2e\_config.json} in the output directory before any work starts. Every step writes a completion
marker (\texttt{done.json}) next to its output, so an interrupted run resumes where it stopped and
\texttt{--force step} recomputes a single step. The times below were measured on the RTX PRO 6000.

\subsection{Preparing the input (17 s)}
The capture adapter reads the calibration for all 36 cameras, decodes frame 0 of every video with
ffmpeg (Lanczos resampling from $3840\times2160$ to the $1920\times1080$ working resolution), and
removes lens distortion with OpenCV so every camera becomes an ideal pinhole camera. Every third
view is copied as the 12 training images \texttt{cam\_00}\ldots\texttt{cam\_11}; all 36 undistorted
frame-0 images are kept for held-out evaluation. The adapter also writes the 12-camera and
36-camera calibration files used by the rest of the pipeline.

and later steps only read those files, so the steps are independent programs joined by files.

Training is the only substantial cost. The detector, SAM2, and MASt3R networks are freed from GPU
memory once their steps finish, so none of them competes with Gaussian training for memory.

\subsection{Stage B, part 1: decoding the sequence}
Before tracking starts, Stage B prepares all per-frame data. Six worker processes each stream one
camera's video through ffmpeg and apply exactly the frame-0 preprocessing: the same resampling, the
same precomputed undistortion map, and the same per-camera colour correction. The 12 training
cameras are decoded at every frame (8400 images for 700 frames). The 24 held-out cameras are
decoded only at every tenth frame, because they are used only for scoring. Frames are stored as
lightly compressed PNG files, and already decoded frames are skipped on a rerun.

SAM2 then runs once per training camera in video mode. It is prompted with that camera's frame-0
player labels from Stage A and follows every player through the whole sequence. These raw masks
are not trusted as they are: SAM2 treats each camera separately and can swap two players in one
camera but not another. Identities are therefore repaired in 3D during tracking (below). Finally,
the ball is detected and triangulated at frame 0, tracked causally through the sequence with a
gravity and bounce model, and added to the canonical scene as its own small group of Gaussians.

\subsection{Stage B, part 2: the per-frame loop}
The canonical Gaussians are loaded onto the GPU once and stay there for the whole sequence. Control
points are placed on every group (1024 per player, 16 on the ball, 1000 on the background), and each
Gaussian is bound to its eight nearest same-group controls. Then, for every frame $f$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Load.} A background thread has already read frame $f$'s 12 training images from disk
while the GPU was busy with frame $f-1$. The images are downsampled to the tracking resolution
($960\times540$) and uploaded; the full-resolution copies are kept for refinement.
\item \textbf{Masks and anchors (every fifth frame).} The SAM2 masks for frame $f$ are read, their
identities are rebuilt in 3D exactly as in Stage A's \texttt{instances} step, and the repaired masks
are matched to the tracked players by position and jersey colour. Triangulating each player's mask
centre gives a motion anchor. On other frames, no masks or anchors are used.
\item \textbf{Deform.} Fifty Adam iterations update each player's shared translation and local
control offsets; the fused CUDA MLS operation moves the Gaussians. Background controls stay frozen,
and the moving players are rendered over a cached background image.
\item \textbf{Improve detail.} On nine frames out of ten, 100 iterations refine only the crops
around moving players. Every tenth frame is a keyframe. First, a health check compares each
player's rendered footprint with the repaired masks; a player whose overlap has fallen below
0.10 IoU is re-initialized from its canonical shape at its anchor. Then 500 full-image iterations
run with limited densification (at most 5\% more Gaussians per keyframe, and never more than 2 million
in total), the Gaussians are re-bound to their controls, and the background
cache is refreshed.
\item \textbf{Record.} The current state is rendered for held-out views 13 and 26 and for the orbit
camera and streamed to ffmpeg, so each video frame shows the scene at that instant. Every tenth
frame, and at the last frame, all 24 held-out cameras are rendered and scored (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS,
and player-region PSNR); the training views also report mask IoU. Every 25 frames, the full
Gaussian state is saved as a PLY checkpoint and the control trajectories and metrics are flushed
to disk.
\end{enumerate}
After the last frame, the final state is saved and per-stage timings and quality statistics are
summarized in \texttt{summary.json}. The held-out view-13 video and the orbit video are then
published to \texttt{final\_renders/} with an \texttt{animated\_} prefix, marking them as renders
of the moving scene rather than of one baked checkpoint.

\subsection{A failure that only appears late in the video}
Development runs covered at most 300 frames. The first full 700-frame run on the RTX PRO 6000
revealed a problem that shorter runs could not show (Figure~\ref{fig:drift}, orange). Each keyframe
may add up to 5\% more Gaussians to add detail. That rule compounds: after 70 keyframes the scene had
grown from 0.74 million to 6.86 million Gaussians. The extra Gaussians were fitted to the 12 training
cameras only, so from the test cameras they appeared as dark floaters and smears across the court and
the stands nearest the camera (Figure~\ref{fig:uncapped-grid}). Test PSNR fell from 20.8~dB to 16.1~dB.

We tested three fixes on the first 300 frames before choosing one. Freezing the background at
keyframes was about 2~dB \emph{worse} from frame 50 onward: keyframes are genuinely useful for the
background, for example to fill in court that was hidden behind a player at frame 0. Capping the
total size worked. Each keyframe may still add up to 5\%, but the scene may never grow past a fixed
total. A first cap at 1.5 times the frame-0 size (1.1 million Gaussians) already ended at 19.3~dB
instead of 16.1~dB (Figure~\ref{fig:drift}, blue), and was faster (3.71 instead of 4.87~s per frame),
used less GPU memory (7.2 instead of 12.1~GB), and saved a 114~MB instead of a 714~MB final scene.

\paragraph{How large should the budget be?} The 1.1 million cap fixes the collapse but leaves
players short of detail, so we compared budgets of 2 and 3 million Gaussians over the whole video
(Table~\ref{tab:budget}). The 3 million run was started from the 2 million run's saved state at
frame 250, before the two differ, instead of from frame 0. \textbf{2 million is the best overall
and is the final method}: it has the best test PSNR (20.43~dB), SSIM and LPIPS, it ends at 19.6~dB
instead of 19.3~dB (Figure~\ref{fig:drift}, green dotted line), and it costs 15\% more tracking
time than 1.1 million. Going to 3 million improves players by another 0.3~dB, but the background
starts to break up again: test PSNR in the second half falls from 20.03 to 19.40~dB, and the worst
test view at frame 650 drops from 18.6 to 16.5~dB with visible floaters. The uncapped run still has
the best player score, because its players keep receiving new Gaussians, but its background falls
apart.

\paragraph{Are players sharper with 3 million Gaussians?} In the videos, players in the 3 million run
can look sharper and more solid, and the player score in Table~\ref{tab:budget} agrees (23.31 against
23.02~dB). That score, however, is an average over test cameras. Scoring each person separately, on
exactly the same pixels for both runs (Table~\ref{tab:per-player}), gives the opposite answer: the
2 million run is better for 14 of the 15 people and objects, and by 0.57~dB overall. Both are correct,
because they weigh cameras differently. Averaging per camera rewards the 3 million run for looking
better in most views (it wins on 31 of 44 test frames); pooling every player pixel penalizes it for
a few views where players are badly wrong, usually where a returning floater crosses a player. In
short, 3 million gives sharper players in typical views, but less reliable players and a worse
background, so 2 million remains the better overall choice.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and deliverables}
The 12 training cameras are never used to judge quality. At every tenth frame and at the last frame,
the tracker renders all 24 test cameras and compares them with the real images from those cameras.
Nothing about the method was tuned on the test cameras except the growth cap, which was chosen from a
short 300-frame comparison; its effect on the full video is reported above.

The run writes two animated videos while it tracks: a test-camera video (view 13) and a camera that
orbits the court. Each video frame is drawn from the scene exactly as it was at that moment of the
game. The final results folder also keeps the editable frame-0 scene (79~MB), the control-point paths
for all 700 frames (113~MB), and the final scene state (208~MB, 2.0 million Gaussians). A single
saved state is one instant of the game, not the whole video: storing every frame naively would take
about $700\times208$~MB $\approx$ 146~GB, so a practical package would store the frame-0 scene plus the
per-frame motion in compressed form.

\section{Inference: showing the reconstruction to a viewer}\label{sec:inference}
Reconstruction (Stages A and B) is the slow, one-off part. Showing the result needs no training and
none of the neural networks used earlier (SAM2, MASt3R, the person detector). To draw one moment of
the game from any camera, only two steps run on the GPU:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Bake} the moment: move every Gaussian to where it is at that frame. The control points
store the motion; one fused CUDA kernel applies it (the same MLS step used during tracking).
\item \textbf{Draw}: evaluate each Gaussian's colour for the viewing direction and rasterize (splat)
the Gaussians into the image.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What has to be stored}
A saved scene is one instant of the game. To play the whole video, the simplest option is to store a
full scene for every frame, but at 2 million Gaussians that is about 200~MB per frame in our current
PLY format, or 140~GB for 700 frames. It is tempting to store the scene once and then only the
control-point positions for each frame, because the control points carry the motion. We tested this
directly: starting from the saved scene at frame 600 and moving it with the stored control points,
every player lands within 0.013 scene units of where the full run placed it at frame 650 (players had
moved up to 11 units). The motion is therefore captured almost exactly. The picture is not: the full
run also updates colours and adds detail at every keyframe and refines players every frame, and
without those updates the test-camera PSNR is 0.6~dB lower after 10 frames and 1.4~dB lower after 50.

A faithful and compact package therefore stores three things:
\begin{itemize}
\item the background once, plus the few background changes made at keyframes;
\item the players' Gaussians at every keyframe (every 10 frames; about 193{,}000 Gaussians, under
10\% of the scene);
\item the control-point positions for every frame (15{,}352 points, about 92~kB per frame at
half precision), which move the players between keyframes.
\end{itemize}
The Gaussians themselves can be made much smaller. Our PLY files spend about 104 bytes per Gaussian
on full-precision numbers and unused fields. Half precision alone halves that. Vector quantization
goes further: instead of storing every Gaussian's scale, rotation and colour, it stores a small
``codebook'' of typical values and, per Gaussian, the index of the closest one. Published methods for
static scenes report 15--50$\times$ smaller files at almost the same quality
(LightGaussian~\cite{fan2024lightgaussian}, CompGS~\cite{navaneet2024compgs}); QUEEN applies the same
idea to per-frame changes in video~\cite{girish2024queen}. We have not yet compressed our scenes, so
the following numbers are \emph{estimates} from those published ratios: roughly 5--10~MB for the
background, under 1~MB per keyframe of players, and the control points, which is about
10--35~Mbit/s depending on how well the control motion is compressed. That is the same range as a
single 4K video stream.

\subsection{Two ways to serve viewers}
Delivering this to many people is closer to streaming a video game than to running a neural
network, and the two standard game-streaming designs both apply.
\paragraph{Render on the server (like cloud gaming).} The viewer sends where their virtual camera
is; a server GPU bakes and draws that view, encodes it with the GPU's video encoder, and streams
ordinary video back. Any device can watch, and the bandwidth is that of normal video. The costs are
a network round trip whenever the viewer moves the camera (tens of milliseconds) and GPU time per
viewer. Three things make this efficient at scale: bake each moment once and share it between all
viewers watching that moment (baking is per game frame, drawing is per viewer); draw several
viewers' cameras in one rasterizer call; and hand finished frames straight from GPU memory to the
video encoder. Our current exporter instead copies every frame to the CPU and pipes it to ffmpeg,
which is now its slowest step.
\paragraph{Render on the viewer's device (like downloading game assets).} The server only sends
the compressed package above, and the viewer's GPU bakes and draws. Camera moves are instant and
server cost is little more than a content-delivery network, but the viewer needs a capable GPU:
2 million Gaussians is heavy for a phone. The usual fix from games is level of detail: send fewer,
larger Gaussians for distant or less important parts of the scene. A service would likely use both,
with server rendering for weak devices and local rendering for strong ones.

\subsection{Why ``just compile it'' does not work here}
Tools such as TensorRT speed up neural networks by optimizing a fixed graph of large, regular
operations (matrix multiplications, convolutions) whose sizes are known in advance. Gaussian
splatting is not like that. Every frame it projects the Gaussians, sorts them by depth within each
small screen tile, and blends them front to back, and how many Gaussians land in each tile changes
with every camera and every moment of the game. The work is dominated by sorting and irregular
memory access in custom CUDA kernels, which such compilers cannot express or improve. Speed-ups
therefore come from engineering the renderer itself, as game engines do:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fuse steps.} Baking is already a single kernel; folding the colour evaluation into it
would remove another pass over all Gaussians.
\item \textbf{Remove launch overhead} by recording the whole bake-and-draw sequence once as a CUDA
graph and replaying it every frame.
\item \textbf{Reuse work across frames.} Consecutive frames are nearly identical, so the previous
depth order is an excellent starting point for the next sort.
\item \textbf{Skip what is not visible}: drop Gaussians outside the camera's view before sorting,
and use coarser detail far away.
\item \textbf{Port the renderer} to Vulkan or WebGPU compute shaders so it runs in game engines and
web browsers, where client-side viewing would happen.
\end{itemize}

\section{Can the method use fewer cameras?}
We did not rebuild the full pipeline with fewer than 12 cameras, but two measurements indicate where
the limit is. First, when one training camera was removed (leaving a $60^\circ$ gap), player PSNR
seen from that gap fell from 18.3 to 17.0~dB, so a single wider gap already costs about 1.3~dB.
Second, the method finds each player in 3D by asking at least four cameras to agree. With 12 cameras
($30^\circ$ apart) most players are seen by enough cameras; with 6 ($60^\circ$ apart) many players
would be seen well by only two or three, and identities would start to fail. Our estimate is that
9 cameras ($40^\circ$ apart) is the practical minimum for this design, at a cost of roughly 1~dB,
while 6 would need a stronger shape prior, for example a learned human body model, to replace the
missing views.

\section{Limitations and next steps}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Players in crowded plays.} Player tracking still degrades late in the video. The
training-camera mask overlap falls from 0.52 at frame 100 to about 0.16 after frame 600, and the
method needed 205 recoveries, where a lost player is reset from its frame-0 shape. Player PSNR drops
from 24.30~dB in the first half to 21.78~dB in the second. The next step is to reduce these
recoveries, for example by tracking players jointly when they overlap instead of one at a time.
\item \textbf{A few thin floaters.} Some long, thin Gaussians near the cameras still show up as
streaks in a few test views. Limiting how elongated a new Gaussian may become would target them.
\item \textbf{Data preparation time.} Decoding and masking take almost as long as tracking at
1080p. The faster data path used for 4K should be made the default at every resolution.
\item \textbf{Storage.} A compact package (frame-0 scene plus compressed per-frame motion) is needed
to ship the whole video instead of one state per frame.
\end{itemize}
